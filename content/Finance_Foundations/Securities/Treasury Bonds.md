It is a form of [[Loans and Interests|loan]] where you lend money to an entity for a set period of time with a fixed or variable interest rate. It is a form of [[Simple Discount|discount]].

Bonds give you a **claim**, you are the creditor.

There are two types of bonds:
- **Government Bonds** - safe discount loan sold to a government. Also called as T-bonds.^def-government-bonds
- **Corporate Bonds** - risky discount loan sold to a company.^def-corporation-bonds

**Note**: Assume the entity is the US government from this moment forward.

## Characteristics:
- **Value** - based on market interest rate of the economy, the rate of a bond is called a **coupon rate**^def-coupon-rate
	- **Interest Rate Risk** - small changes in the market changes a bond's current market price. Hence, governments add additional return called  **Term Premium**^term-premium
- **Maturity** - long-term, maturing in 20 to 30 years.
- **Historical performance** - higher [[Return|return]] than [[Treasury Bills]], look at [[Historical Evidence|historical evidence]]
- **Certainty** - [[Treasury Bills]] fluctuate in price and carry risk in unknown price in the future, but t-bonds are stable long term and are valued more for some for this property of being certain.

They can be thought of as [[Treasury Bills]] with longer riskier maturity dates.
# Mechanism behind Price
Bonds are *constantly compared* to new ones issued by an entity and the current market rate.
- When market interest rates go up from 5% to 7%, then the government has to no choice but to increase the price of the bond as well.
- Thus, current existing bonds decrease in worth compared to newly issued ones. Note that the price is the same.

T-bonds have an *inverse relationship in price with the economic inflation*. 

Whether a 5% bond is "good" or "bad" depends entirely on the **Opportunity Cost**:

| If Market Rate is... | 5% Bond is...                  | Price of your Bond will... |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------ | -------------------------- |
| 7% (Higher)          | Less attractive (Lower yield)  | **Decrease** (Discount)    |
| 3% (Lower)           | More attractive (Higher yield) | **Increase** (Premium)     |
| 5% (Same)            | Fairly valued                  | Stay at **Par** ($1,000)   |

# Value in the Loan Relationship
Usually there are two types of investors:
- **Maximize value** - they care towards the current price of bond and so changes hands or keeps it according to how much they are willing to take in risk in a given period of time; 
- **Minimize unknown risk** - some value more for the long term certainty in its security by the government.

## Utility by the Issuer
T-Bonds exist as governments needs to lock in stable investments. 
- **If**: the major portion of the national budget is based on t-bills
- **Then**: It is a logistic and administrative nightmare for the national budget.
- **For**: It is at the mercy of weekly interest rate spikes because of changes in national inflation.

## Utility by the Creditor
These are often bought by Insurance Companies and other Social Security institutions as they need the discounts' **certainty** when paying out millions to retirees. 

# Instances

## Government Bonds
- **Issuers**: these are done by sovereign states.
	- **US equivalent**: Treasury notes (2-10 years) and Treasury bonds (20-30 years).
	- **PH equivalent**: Fixed Rate Treasury Notes (FXTNs) and Retail Treasury Bonds (RTBs).
- **Goal**: used to fund public infrastructure or pay off national debt.
- **Risk**: they are considered safe since the government can raise taxes to pay lenders.

## Corporate Bonds
- **Issuers**: companies (Apple, San Miguel Corporation) issue bonds.
- **Goal**: when it needs to build a new factory or buy another company.
- **Risk**: riskier since a company can go bankrupt, so it has a higher coupon rate to attract investors called **Credit Risk Premium**^credit-risk-premium
